Headline

Missed the departure deadline? The BIA shuts the door for 10 years

In Matter of Ontiveros-Diaz (30 I&N Dec. 222), the Board holds that a motion to reopen with new evidence does not erase a voluntary departure violation.

Voluntary departure is a bargain: the noncitizen avoids a formal removal order and, in exchange, promises to leave the United States within the period set by the judge. Breaking that promise is costly — INA 240B(d)(1) makes the person ineligible for 10 years for cancellation of removal, adjustment of status, change of status and other benefits. In the case decided on November 5, 2026, the respondent did not depart on time and later sought to reopen to present new evidence on a cancellation application that had already been denied, including records about his children's special needs. The Board denied the motion: the statute bars "any further relief" after the violation, and a motion filed after the departure period does not affect the time allowed for departure (8 CFR 1240.26(e)(2)). In the alternative, the Board found the new evidence merely supplemental and unlikely to change the outcome. The practical lesson is about the calendar: anyone who intends to challenge the decision must act within the voluntary departure period — and anyone who will not leave must understand the consequences of staying before the deadline passes.

Official source: Matter of Ontiveros-Diaz, 30 I&N Dec. 222 (BIA 2026), Interim Decision #4272 ↗

Immigration Courts

BIA precedent

Bond: the judge must explain which version of the facts he credited

In Matter of G-M-T-, 30 I&N Dec. 228 (Interim Decision #4273), the Board holds that when a party offers an alternative proffer on a fact material to bond — or challenges a proffer as uncorroborated — the immigration judge must address the dispute and give a reasoned explanation in the bond memorandum for the weight given to each account. For detained clients this is a concrete procedural safeguard: uncorroborated assertions, from either side, cannot simply be adopted without analysis, and a missing explanation opens the door to appeal.

Official source: Matter of G-M-T-, 30 I&N Dec. 228 (BIA 2026) ↗ · EOIR — Volume 30 ↗
BIA precedent

When no bond is enough: Matter of Cruz Aijiaco

In the preceding decision in the series, Matter of Cruz Aijiaco, 30 I&N Dec. 218 (Interim Decision #4271), the Board concluded that no amount of bond suffices given multiple motor vehicle violations, no fixed address and an apparent lack of eligibility for any relief. Read together with G-M-T-, the two decisions sketch today's custody hearings: the Board will accept a denial of release based on history and lack of ties, but requires the judge to show the reasoning behind it. For the defense, the priority is documenting address, family ties and the relief actually available.

Official source: Matter of Cruz Aijiaco, 30 I&N Dec. 218 (BIA 2026) ↗

Rules & Agencies

Proposed rule

Friday closes comments on ending deference to prior approvals

DHS proposes to remove 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5), the rule adopted in the December 2024 H-1B modernization under which an I-129 extension involving the same parties and facts starts from the prior approval, absent material error, a material change or new information. Without it, every H-1B, L-1, O-1 and similar extension would be reviewed from scratch, with the full burden of proof on the employer — DHS itself estimates about 6,000 additional requests for evidence per year. Comments may be submitted on Regulations.gov (docket USCIS-2026-0529) through November 13, 2026. Employers with extensions due in 2027 should plan their documentation as if deference no longer exists.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20941 ↗
Proposed rule

The 60-day grace period: comments closed, now it is DHS's move

Yesterday, November 10, the comment window closed on the proposal that would eliminate the up-to-60 days that workers who lose their jobs in H-1B, L-1, O-1, TN, E-1, E-2 or E-3 status have to change employers, change status or depart (8 CFR 214.1(l)(2)). Next comes DHS's review of the comments and, if it proceeds, a final rule in the Federal Register with its own effective date. Until then, the grace period remains fully in force.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-18631 ↗
Final rule

EB-5: 19 days until the new fee schedule

USCIS's final rule raises EB-5 fees as of November 30: Form I-526, for example, goes from $3,675 to $7,615 including the technology fee, the initial I-956 fee rises to $44,115, and the I-526E Integrity Fund fee goes from $1,000 to $1,100. Petitions postmarked by November 29 pay the current schedule; for investors whose documents are ready, filing early means direct savings.

In Perspective

Veterans Day: the citizenship fast track for those who wore the uniform

Today the United States honors its veterans — and few remember that immigration law reserves for them one of the most generous paths to citizenship. Under INA 328, a person who served honorably for at least one year in the Armed Forces may naturalize without meeting the usual residence and physical presence periods, provided he or she is a permanent resident. INA 329 goes further: for service during periods of hostilities designated by presidential order, even the green card is not required in certain cases, and there is no minimum length of service — honorable service for any period may suffice. The naturalization filing fee is waived in these cases, and INA 329A even allows posthumous citizenship for those who died as a result of their service. The lesson for this moment is that, even amid increasingly restrictive rules, Congress has preserved an old principle: those who risk their lives for the country should not wait in line to be called citizens.

Official source: USCIS — Citizenship for Military Personnel & Family Members ↗ · INA 328, 329 and 329A (8 U.S.C. 1439, 1440, 1440-1).
