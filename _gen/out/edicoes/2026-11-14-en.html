Today's lead · Weekend edition

Asylum, bond and voluntary departure: the BIA tightens the screws

Ontiveros-Diaz (ID 4272), G-M-T- (ID 4273) and A-C-V-M- (ID 4274): voluntary departure, bond and gender-based asylum — what the new precedents change for people in removal proceedings.

The Board of Immigration Appeals has issued three precedent decisions in recent days, binding on immigration judges nationwide. In Matter of Ontiveros-Diaz, 30 I&N Dec. 222, the Board held that violating a voluntary departure agreement makes the person ineligible for certain immigration benefits for 10 years, and that filing a motion to reopen to submit new evidence on a prior cancellation-of-removal application does not lift that consequence. Matter of G-M-T-, 30 I&N Dec. 228, is procedural and helps the defense: when a party offers an alternative account of a fact material to bond, or challenges the government's proffer as uncorroborated, the judge must address it and explain in the bond memorandum the weight given to each account. In Matter of A-C-V-M-, 30 I&N Dec. 236, the Board rejected “Guatemalan women without partners” as a particular social group for lack of particularity and social distinction, adding that an imputed opinion about social conventions, unrelated to the government, is not an imputed political opinion. Taken together, the three decisions point the same way: removal proceedings are less forgiving of missed deadlines and loosely drawn theories — but more demanding of the judge's reasoning when the defense brings evidence. If you were granted voluntary departure, leave on time or seek the appropriate relief before the deadline runs; if you are seeking asylum, the social group must be carefully defined and proven, ideally alongside alternative grounds.

Official source: EOIR — Precedent Decisions, Volume 30 (IDs 4272, 4273 and 4274) ↗

Rules & Agencies

Proposed rule

Deference and refugees: comments closed, the ball is in DHS's court

Comment periods on two DHS proposals closed on Friday, November 13. The first (FR 2026-20941) would delete the 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5) rule requiring USCIS to defer to a prior approval when extending H-1B, L-1, O-1 and other I-129 classifications involving the same parties and facts. The second (FR 2026-20956) removes the 30-day notice to refugees before termination of status and cuts the response time to 10 days. Next comes review of the comments and, possibly, a final rule; until then, current rules remain in force — and employers with extensions due in 2027 should build each filing as if it were the first.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20941 ↗ · Federal Register 2026-20956 ↗
Proposed rule

The 60-day grace period: the decision still to come

The proposal to eliminate the discretionary grace period of up to 60 days (FR 2026-18631) is past its comment phase, which closed November 10. Today, someone who loses an H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-1/E-2/E-3 or TN job may remain up to 60 days (or until the end of validity, if shorter) to change employers or status; without it, the window to react disappears. Until a final rule appears in the Federal Register, the grace period remains available — and workers in these categories should have a plan B ready before any layoff.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-18631 — Eliminating the Discretionary 60-Day Grace Period ↗
Final rule

EB-5: two weeks left on the old fee schedule

New EB-5 fees apply to petitions postmarked on or after Monday, November 30: the I-526 rises from $3,675 to $7,615 (including the technology fee), the initial I-956 fee climbs to $44,115, and the I-526E Integrity Fund contribution goes from $1,000 to $1,100. Investors with a ready file save more than $3,900 on the I-526 alone by mailing by November 29.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20016 ↗

PERM & Labor Certification

Final rule

DOL's administrative law judges move to mandatory e-filing

The Department of Labor finalized (FR 2026-22761, published November 9) its 2021 proposal making electronic filing mandatory in proceedings before the Office of Administrative Law Judges (OALJ), with exceptions for good cause and for self-represented parties. The change takes effect December 9, 2026, and amends the rules of practice at 29 CFR part 18. The OALJ hears, among other matters, H-1B enforcement cases (LCA conditions) and H-2A/H-2B cases; employers and counsel with matters there should have e-filing access set up before that date.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-22761 — Rules of Practice and Procedure for Administrative Hearings Before the OALJ ↗
Alert

PERM in about 10 months; PWD working on August

DOL data through October 31 show an average of 300 days for PERM cases decided in the last 30 days (315 over 90 days; 360 over 180 days), with the National Prevailing Wage Center processing PERM requests received in August 2026 (OEWS). Together, employers should plan for a little over a year from PWD request to certification — and for an H-1B worker nearing year six, PERM must be filed at least 365 days ahead to support extensions beyond the cap.

Official source: DOL/OFLC — FLAG Processing Times (data through 10/31/2026) ↗

Consular & Passports

In effect

A U.S. passport online — even the first one

On Monday, November 16, the State Department launches a pilot accepting certain Form DS-11 passport applications online — the form used for first passports and by those who cannot renew by mail. Eligibility is checked at America.gov, and the pool of eligible applicants is expected to grow as testing progresses; the pilot runs through November 20, 2027, unless extended. For newly naturalized citizens, who need a first passport to travel as Americans, this may be the fastest route — check eligibility before booking an in-person appointment.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23317 — Pilot for Online Version of Application for a U.S. Passport (Form DS-11) ↗

In Perspective

Matter of Lozada: when the lawyer is to blame

Ontiveros-Diaz reminded us a few days ago that reopening a case does not erase everything that came before. But for nearly four decades the BIA has recognized one specific door for reopening cases lost through someone else's fault: ineffective assistance of counsel. In Matter of Lozada (1988), the Board set three requirements that became ritual in every such motion — an affidavit from the noncitizen detailing the agreement with counsel, proof that counsel was informed of the allegations and given a chance to respond, and proof of a bar complaint (or an explanation of why none was filed). In January 2009, the Attorney General in Matter of Compean replaced that test with a stricter one and declared there was no constitutional right to effective counsel in removal proceedings; months later, the new Attorney General vacated the decision entirely and restored Lozada (Compean II). The episode teaches that reopening is a narrow tool, heavy with formal requirements — and that in immigration, even the rules about lawyers' mistakes can change with a new Attorney General.

Official source: Matter of Lozada, 19 I&N Dec. 637 (BIA 1988) ↗ · Matter of Compean, 24 I&N Dec. 710 (A.G. 2009) ↗ · Matter of Compean, Bangaly & J-E-C-, 25 I&N Dec. 1 (A.G. 2009) ↗
