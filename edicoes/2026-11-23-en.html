Today's Lead · PERM & Labor Certification

The PERM overhaul faces the very backlog it promises to shrink

The Labor Department's latest figures show PERM running at an average of 300 days in analyst review, with more than 42,000 cases still pending from August through October alone — and it is that pile-up that the largest rewrite of the program in twenty years, now open for comment through January 19, says it means to attack. While the text is only a proposal, nothing has changed in 20 CFR 656; but the window to shape the outcome is open, and it will close.

The operational picture of PERM, in the Labor Department's own most recent snapshot (data as of October 31), is of a line that will not yield. Average analyst review stands at 300 days over the last 30 days, 315 over the last 90 and 360 over the last 180 — meaning an employer who files today should count on roughly ten months for the labor-certification stage alone, before even reaching the I-140 and adjustment of status. The pending volume is substantial: in the three most recent filing months alone there are 14,925 cases from August, 14,400 from September and 13,179 from October still awaiting review — more than 42,000 cases across ninety days of filings. The prevailing-wage stage, which precedes PERM, is processing August requests on the OEWS basis and July requests off it.

It is against that backdrop that the PERM modernization proposal runs (FR 2026-23370; 91 FR 73916; RIN 1205-AC29; docket ETA-2026-0133), published on November 17 — the biggest revision of the program since electronic PERM was created in 2005. Nothing changes today: 20 CFR Part 656 remains in force. But the proposed text redesigns the labor-market test end to end: it ends the print-newspaper advertisement and replaces it with a single electronic recruitment of at least 90 days; it creates a two-year bar for an employer that has laid off U.S. workers in the same or a similar occupation at the worksite, with a duty to consider the laid-off worker through I-140 approval and to report the layoff to DOL and DHS within five business days; and it eliminates Schedule A.

For the employer the practical reading is twofold. In the short run, today's timelines call for planning with room to spare: an employment-based green card for 2027 has to be born on the 2026 calendar, not at the last minute. In the medium run, the proposal is the moment to act while it is still a proposal: the comment period, running 60 days from November 17, closes on January 19, 2027, through regulations.gov. Anyone planning sponsorships should, starting now, map their own layoff history and use the formal voice while the window is open — once it closes, only the text the DOL chooses to keep will remain.

Rules & Agencies

Final rule

EB-5: one week to the new fees, and the postmark is what decides

The EB-5 fee final rule (FR 2026-20016; 91 FR 61940; RIN 1615-AC93; 8 CFR Parts 106 and 216) takes effect next Monday, November 30 — exactly one week from today. What matters most in practice is how it draws the line: it goes by the date of mailing. Anything sent on November 30 or later pays the new amount; anything postmarked before captures the old one. The increase is substantial — USCIS estimates a weighted-average rise of about 70% across the program's roughly 16,600 annual filings, with the Integrity Fund up 10% and a new Form I-527. Investment floors are unchanged: US$1,050,000 in general and US$800,000 in a targeted employment area. For the investor whose case is ready, the conclusion is plain: mailing this week is the difference of thousands of dollars in fees.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20016 — EB-5 Fee Rule (final rule, 91 FR 61940, effective 11/30/2026) ↗
Final rule

BIA appeals: from December 17, summary disposition becomes the default

On December 17 the DOJ/EOIR final rule rewriting the appeals process at the Board of Immigration Appeals takes effect (FR 2026-23320; 91 FR 73876; 8 CFR Parts 1003, 1208 and 1240). It makes summary disposition — a decision by a single member, with no reasoned opinion — the default path rather than the exception; cuts the appeal deadline from 30 to 10 days (except for asylum merits); and treats as waived any argument that does not appear in the Notice of Appeal. The backdrop is the backlog: the Board's pending stock jumped from about 35,800 cases in fiscal 2017 to 325,800 in fiscal 2026. One point is decisive for anyone with a live case: the rule applies to decisions issued on or after December 17, and does not reach back to appeals already pending. In practice, anyone considering an appeal must reach that date with the argument fully formed, because from then on each appeal is born under a faster regime that is less forgiving of a poorly supported brief.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23320 — Appellate Procedures for the Board of Immigration Appeals (final rule, 91 FR 73876, effective 12/17/2026) ↗

PERM & Labor Certification

Final rule

DOL's OALJ: mandatory electronic filing from December 9

On December 9, a Wednesday, the final rule (FR 2026-22761; 91 FR 70790) takes effect that makes electronic filing mandatory in proceedings before the DOL's Office of Administrative Law Judges (OALJ), with exceptions for good cause and for parties without counsel. The OALJ is where H-1B and H-2A/H-2B enforcement disputes and PERM denial appeals (BALCA) are heard. An employer with a case underway, or at risk of one, needs its electronic-filing access configured before the date — missing a defense deadline over a registration glitch is the kind of harm that cannot be undone.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-22761 — OALJ Mandatory Electronic Filing (final rule, 91 FR 70790, effective 12/09/2026) ↗

The numbers behind the line: a 300-day average and prevailing wage still in August

It is worth looking closely at the data the Labor Department posts on its FLAG portal, current through October 31, because they put into numbers what the employer feels in the wait. In PERM analyst review, the average is 300 days over the last 30 days of activity, 315 over the last 90 and 360 over the last 180. In prevailing wage, the DOL is now processing requests filed in August 2026 on the OEWS basis and July 2026 off it; center-director reviews are in September. And the backlog is visible in the most recent PERM filing months: 14,925 cases from August, 14,400 from September and 13,179 from October still await review. For planning, the message is simple: labor certification is, today, the longest stage of the road, and the case timeline has to be drawn from it.

Official source: DOL/OFLC — FLAG Processing Times (PERM and PWD, data as of 10/31/2026) ↗

High Skilled Individuals

Proposed rule

The 60-day grace period: comments closed, the benefit still in force

The DHS proposal to eliminate the discretionary grace period of up to 60 days (FR 2026-18631; 91 FR 57807; RIN 1615-AD22) had its comments closed on November 10, and the Federal Register still carries no final act. Until the final act issues, the benefit stands: under 8 CFR 214.1(l)(2), when employment ends, the holder of an H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-1/E-2/E-3 or TN may remain lawfully in the United States for up to 60 days — or until the end of the validity of status, if that is shorter — to find a new sponsor, change category or arrange departure. With the comment window closed, the text can reappear at any time as a final rule, with an effective date of its own. Until then, every week is time in favor of anyone at risk of a layoff, and the plan B is best built before, not after.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-18631 — Eliminating the Discretionary 60-Day Grace Period (proposed rule, 91 FR 57807; comments closed 11/10/2026) ↗
Proposed rule

Deference to prior approvals: the other undated proposal, also in force

The second undated proposal is the DHS one (FR 2026-20941; 91 FR 65119; RIN 1615-AD26) to revoke 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5) — the rule that today requires USCIS to respect its own prior approval when adjudicating an extension of H-1B, L-1, O-1 and other I-129 categories, where the parties, the facts and the activity are the same. Comments closed on November 13 and, as with the grace period, the final act did not issue: deference remains in force. While it holds, each extension starts from what the agency has already recognized, and a routine renewal tends to be routine. If the proposal becomes a final rule, the officer will be able to re-examine from scratch the eligibility he himself had already approved — and a renewal that looked assured becomes one that can be denied on a full re-examination. The defense is the one from before deference existed: build every extension request with the evidentiary force of a first petition.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20941 — Removing Deference to Prior USCIS Determinations of Eligibility (proposed rule, 91 FR 65119; comments closed 11/13/2026) ↗

In Perspective

The Sunday ad: why PERM required the newspaper page for twenty years — and why the proposal retires it

There is an irony a newspaper will not let pass. Since the rule that created electronic PERM, in 2004, the labor-market test for professional positions has required, among other steps, two advertisements in a newspaper of general circulation, on Sundays (20 CFR 656.17(e)). The choice of Sunday was no accident: it was the day and the medium on which the serious American worker looked for a job, and the print advertisement became the legal stand-in for a genuine search for domestic labor. For two decades, then, immigration practice kept the Sunday classifieds alive — a paper obligation at the heart of an entire program.

Twenty years on, the modernization proposal (FR 2026-23370) would retire the print advertisement and trade it for a single, continuous electronic recruitment of at least 90 days — the acknowledgment, at last, that the labor market has moved to the screen. The deeper point, beyond the curiosity, is that the "labor-market test" has always been a moving target: what counts as a real search for American workers changes with the place where Americans actually look for work. For the reader, the lesson is practical — the yardstick is being redrawn in real time, and the comment window is the one moment the public helps draw the new line. When it closes, on January 19, the yardstick will already be drawn.
