Adjustment of Status — BIA Precedent

The BIA overrules two decisions from 1945 and 1957: an admission of drug use made at a USCIS interview is enough for inadmissibility and bars adjustment of status

In Matter of Mendez Maldonado, 30 I&N Dec. 62 (BIA 2026), Interim Decision No. 4247, decided September 22 and released today through the EOIR library, the Board overrules Matter of K- (1957) and Matter of J- (1945). An alien no longer has to be given the precise statutory definition of the offense, with all essential elements, before making an admission. Today's Federal Register carries no new action in the areas we follow.

The respondent, a native and citizen of Mexico, sought adjustment of status under section 245(a) of the INA. At a USCIS adjustment interview in September 2016 he stated under oath that he bought and used cocaine, monthly, for about two years. The Immigration Judge held that the statement makes him inadmissible under section 212(a)(2)(A)(i)(II) of the INA — which reaches an alien who “admits having committed” or “admits committing acts which constitute the essential elements” of a violation of any law relating to a controlled substance — and therefore ineligible for adjustment. The Board affirmed and dismissed the appeal.

The change is in the reasoning. Since Matter of K-, a valid admission for immigration purposes required that the officer give the alien an adequate definition of the crime, including all essential elements, explained in understandable terms — a standard Matter of J- had broken into six requirements. The Board held that this exceeds the statute, which asks only that the alien admit acts constituting the essential elements of the offense. Citing Garland v. Ming Dai (2021), the Board said it lacks authority to impose admissibility standards beyond those set out in the immigration laws, and expressly overruled both precedents.

Two practical readings. First, the procedural safeguard that for nearly seventy years served to invalidate admissions taken hastily at an interview no longer exists as a general rule; what remains is that the admission be voluntary and the hearing fair. Second, the Board recorded an independent ground: an admission made under oath before the Immigration Judge, with counsel present, stands on its own, regardless of what happened at USCIS. Worth recalling in practice that inadmissibility under section 212(a)(2)(A)(i)(II) requires no conviction — the admission suffices — and that the section 212(h) waiver reaches only simple possession of 30 grams or less of marijuana, with no equivalent for cocaine. The adjustment interview now calls for even more careful preparation.

Inadmissibility & Removal

Precedent

Also in Volume 30: an aggravated felony conviction now creates a freestanding ground of inadmissibility, and Matter of Kanga is overruled

An item this edition had not yet covered. In Matter of A-V-O-, 30 I&N Dec. 44 (BIA 2026), Interim Decision No. 4245, decided September 17, the Board held that an alien convicted of an aggravated felony is inadmissible under section 212(a)(8)(A) of the INA as an alien ineligible to citizenship, and expressly overruled Matter of Kanga, 22 I&N Dec. 1206 (BIA 2000), which had held otherwise.

The practical effect is to add an independent ground of inadmissibility alongside those already arising under section 212(a)(2), with direct consequences for adjustment and admission cases in which the aggravated felony did not fit neatly within the traditional criminal grounds. Cases whose inadmissibility analysis was built on the earlier rule deserve review.

PERM & Labor Certification

In effect

OFLC publishes a records management page and explains how long FLAG keeps case records

A September 17 announcement this edition had not yet recorded. The Office of Foreign Labor Certification published a Records Management page on the foreign labor certification website, covering its records retention policy, how the Foreign Labor Application Gateway (FLAG) retains and disposes of records, and guidance on searching and retrieving case information in the system.

It is not a change of rule, but it matters to PERM and LCA practice: the audit file obligation belongs to the employer, and system retention does not replace it. Older cases one expects to consult in FLAG deserve a copy of their own while still available.

Enforcement & Integrity

Alert

USCIS announces two naturalization-related indictments on September 22

USCIS reported on September 22 that it assisted two criminal investigations that ended in indictment. In the first, two Chinese nationals turned themselves in on September 17 to face, for one of them, a naturalization charge and, for both, a firearm charge, as announced by the U.S. Attorney's Office for the Eastern District of Missouri. In the second, the agency supported the investigation leading to the indictment of Miran Kostic, a national of Bosnia and Herzegovina accused of prisoner abuse and of lying to obtain U.S. citizenship.

The point here is trend, not a change in the rules: the agency continues to publicize, nearly weekly, fraud found in naturalization filings. Consistency between what is declared on each form over the years remains the weakest point in those cases.
