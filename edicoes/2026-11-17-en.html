Today's Lead · PERM & Labor Certification

PERM: the biggest overhaul in twenty years opens for comment

The Department of Labor publishes today the proposal that rebuilds the labor market test at the heart of labor certification: a single recruitment period of at least 90 days, a bar on employers who laid off in the same occupation within the last two years, and the end of Schedule A. Nothing is in effect yet — what begins today is the 60-day clock to comment.

The Department of Labor (DOL), through its Employment and Training Administration, publishes today, Tuesday, November 17, the proposed rule “Modernizing the Labor Market Test and Improving Protections for U.S. Workers in the PERM Immigrant Visa Program” (RIN 1205-AC29). It is the most sweeping revision of permanent labor certification since PERM itself was created nearly twenty years ago, and it strikes at the core of the most common employment path to a green card: the test an employer must pass to prove that no U.S. worker was available, qualified, and willing to take the job.

The first thing to fix in mind is that nothing changes today. This is a proposal, not a final rule: the current regime under 20 CFR Part 656 continues to govern in full until — and unless — a final rule takes effect. What today's publication opens is a 60-day public comment period, closing on January 16, 2027, received through regulations.gov under docket ETA-2026-0133. Anyone with a PERM case on the horizon should use that window: it is the formal chance for the business and legal community to tell DOL what works and what would make sponsorship unworkable.

The proposed design is aggressive on three fronts. Recruitment would stop being a collection of discrete steps anchored by a printed newspaper ad and become a single advertising period of at least 90 calendar days through electronic channels (the employer's website, job boards, social media, job fairs and campus recruiting), with the recruitment report filed with DOL at submission. Alongside it, a two-year bar would arise: an employer that has laid off U.S. workers in the same occupation — or a similar one — at the place of employment in the two years before the request would be blocked from filing. And Schedule A — the shortcut that waived recruitment for professional nurses, physical therapists, and people of exceptional ability in the sciences and arts — would be eliminated, pushing those occupations back into the full process.

The practical reading for employers is twofold. For cases that must move now, today's rule is still the familiar one — and a PERM filed under the current system remains governed by it. But anyone mapping a green-card calendar for 2027 and beyond should prepare for a labor market test that is longer, more heavily documented, and gated by a layoff screen that could, on its own, foreclose sponsorship. It is worth starting now to map the company's layoff history in the same occupation and to rethink timelines in light of a recruitment that may run well past three months.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23370 — Modernizing the Labor Market Test and Improving Protections for U.S. Workers in the PERM Immigrant Visa Program ↗ · Comments: regulations.gov, docket ETA-2026-0133 ↗

PERM & Labor Certification

Proposed rule

The two-year bar: layoffs block the filing — and the duty to the laid-off worker outlasts it

The most sensitive point for employers is the layoff bar. Under the proposal, a PERM could not be filed if U.S. workers in the same occupation (or a similar one) at the place of employment were laid off in the two years before the request — a look-back counted backward from the date certification is sought. And the duty would not end at filing: the employer would carry a continuing obligation to consider and offer the position to potentially qualified U.S. workers who were laid off, tracking the case through I-140 approval; a later layoff would have to be reported to DOL — and to DHS, if an I-140 is pending — within five business days. In practice, the decision to sponsor would speak directly to the company's workforce policy.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23370 — PERM Modernization NPRM ↗
Proposed rule

End of Schedule A: nurses and physical therapists would return to the full process

The same proposal would eliminate Schedule A, the list that today pre-recognizes a shortage in certain occupations and waives the employer's recruitment and individual DOL certification — the classic shortcut for professional nurses, physical therapists, and people of exceptional ability in the sciences or arts. Without it, those positions would have to pass the full labor market test before the employer could file the immigrant petition. For hospitals and clinics that built their international hiring pipeline around Schedule A, it is the change felt fastest — and one of the points the comment window is likely to heat up.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23370 — PERM Modernization NPRM ↗
Final rule

DOL's OALJ: mandatory electronic filing begins December 9

While the great PERM overhaul is only beginning to be debated, one change is already certain and near: on December 9, the DOL final rule (FR 2026-22761) takes effect making electronic filing mandatory in proceedings before the Office of Administrative Law Judges (OALJ), with good-cause exceptions and an exception for self-represented parties. OALJ is where H-1B and H-2A/H-2B enforcement disputes are heard; an employer with a case underway, or at risk of one, needs access to the electronic system set up before the date, or risk missing filing deadlines.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-22761 — OALJ Mandatory Electronic Filing ↗
Watch

Today's clock: PERM still runs about ten months — and it is the system you plan 2027 around

Until the overhaul becomes a final rule, today's system governs the calendar — and DOL data through October 31 keep the average near 300 days for PERMs decided in the last month, with prevailing-wage determinations (PWD) processing August requests. Adding up the stages of wage determination, recruitment, and review, an employer should count a little over a year between the PWD request and certification. And there is the deadline that gives no grace: to support H-1B extensions beyond the sixth year, the PERM must be filed at least 365 days before the beneficiary exhausts status — a count that a longer recruitment would only tighten.

Rules & Agencies

Proposed rule

The 60-day grace period: still in effect, while no final rule has issued

DHS's proposal to eliminate the discretionary grace period of up to 60 days (FR 2026-18631) remains in the comment-review stage, with comments closed on November 10 and no final rule published. Until the Federal Register carries the definitive act, the benefit still applies: a worker who loses a job in H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-1/E-2/E-3, or TN may remain up to 60 days (or until the end of the status validity, if shorter) to change employers or change category. It is the window that buys time to file a new petition — and, while it is at risk of disappearing, anyone in these categories should keep a plan B ready before any layoff.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-18631 — Eliminating the Discretionary 60-Day Grace Period ↗
Proposed rule

Deference to prior approvals: the wait employers should turn to their advantage

Also awaiting a DHS decision is the proposal (FR 2026-20941) to rescind 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5) — the rule that today directs USCIS to respect its prior approval when adjudicating extensions of H-1B, L-1, O-1, and other I-129 categories where the parties and facts are the same; comments closed on November 13. While deference still applies, each extension still starts from the approval already granted; if the rule becomes final, USCIS could re-examine each request from scratch. The practical reading has not changed: building 2027 extension requests with the evidentiary strength of a first petition is the hedge against both scenarios.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20941 — Deference to Prior Determinations ↗
Final rule

EB-5: under two weeks to file at the old fee schedule

The new fees for the EB-5 investor program apply to petitions postmarked on or after Monday, November 30: the I-526 rises from US$ 3,675 to US$ 7,615 (technology fee included), the initial I-956 fee goes to US$ 44,115, and the Integrity Fund contribution on the I-526E goes from US$ 1,000 to US$ 1,100. An applicant with a completed package saves more than US$ 3,900 on the I-526 alone by filing on or before November 29 — and it is the postmark date, not USCIS receipt, that counts.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20016 — EB-5 Immigrant Investor Program Fees ↗

Consular & Passports

In effect

The first U.S. passport-by-internet pilot is now live

The Department of State's pilot project that accepts certain Form DS-11 applications online — the form used by first-time passport applicants — went live on Monday, November 16. Eligibility is checked electronically at the start of the process, the admitted group is expected to grow as the pilot advances, and the program runs through November 20, 2027. For a newly naturalized citizen who needs a first passport as a U.S. citizen, it may be the fastest route to a first trip — worth checking now whether the application fits the test phase.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23317 — Pilot for Online Version of Application for a U.S. Passport (Form DS-11) ↗

In Perspective

“Twenty years”: why PERM was itself a revolution — and what the labor market test has always tried to measure

When today's proposal is called the biggest change to PERM in nearly twenty years, it is worth remembering that PERM was itself born of a crisis. Until the mid-2000s, labor certification moved on paper and lived buried: stacks of applications sat for years at the State Workforce Agencies, and the old Reduction in Recruitment became a byword for delay. On December 27, 2004, the Department of Labor published in the Federal Register the system it named PERM — Program Electronic Review Management — effective March 28, 2005: standardized recruitment before filing, an electronic form (the ETA-9089), and the promise of decisions in months rather than years. The idea that runs through those twenty years is always the same: before giving a foreign national the path to a green card, the employer must prove, through a real labor market test, that it made a good-faith effort to hire a U.S. worker and did not find one available, qualified, and willing. What changes from regime to regime is how much that test demands, how much it documents, and whom it protects. Today's proposal is the next round of that same question — and that is why it deserves to be read for what it says about the balance between opening the door to foreign talent and keeping a seat for the local worker, not merely for the extra work it hands HR.

Official source: Federal Register, 69 FR 77326 (December 27, 2004) — creation of PERM (effective 03/28/2005) ↗
