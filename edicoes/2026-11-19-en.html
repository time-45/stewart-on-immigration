Today's lead · Rules & Agencies

EB-5: the new fee schedule takes effect on November 30

The final rule that adjusts the fees for the immigrant-investor visa takes effect on November 30 — and it is measured by the postmark. On average, the program's fees rise by about 70%, and anyone who files on or after that date pays the new amount. Eleven days to go.

The Department of Homeland Security, through U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS), published in the Federal Register the final rule “U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services Employment-Based Immigrant Visa, Fifth Preference (EB-5) Fee Rule” (FR 2026-20016; 91 FR 61940; RIN 1615-AC93), effective November 30, 2026. The rule adjusts every EB-5 petition fee — the program that grants a green card to a foreign national who invests in a U.S. enterprise and creates ten jobs — codifies the requirements of the EB-5 Reform and Integrity Act of 2022, creates a technology fee, and establishes the Integrity Fund fees and penalties. The rule is explicit about the trigger: “any application, petition, or request postmarked on or after this date must be accompanied by the fees established by this final rule.” In other words, anyone who manages to file — by postmark — on or before November 29 still pays the current schedule.

The size of the jump is the news of the day. By the agency's own estimate, the rule reaches about 16,600 EB-5 filings a year and raises the average fee by roughly 70.7% — an increase on the order of US$2,945.90 per filing on a weighted-average basis. The Integrity Fund fees rise 10% as an inflation adjustment: the I-526E fee goes from US$1,000 to US$1,100; the regional-center annual fee from US$10,000 to US$11,000; and, for larger regional centers, from US$20,000 to US$22,000. The full, form-by-form schedule appears in the new Form G-1055 included in the docket, and the adjustment comes alongside the creation of the new Form I-527.

There is a reason the increase looks so abrupt, and it lies within the rule itself. On November 12, 2025, the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado, in Moody v. Noem, stayed the EB-5 fees DHS had set in its 2024 general fee schedule, holding that the EB-5 Reform Act barred the agency from adjusting the program's fees that way. DHS disagrees, but complied: since November 12, 2025, USCIS reverted to the EB-5 fees in effect until March 2024 — in practice, the 2016 amounts. It is against that older baseline, then, that the new schedule is measured; today's adjustment resets the fees to a level calculated specifically for the program's cost, under an EB-5-specific fee study.

For decision-makers, the takeaway is concrete. What changes is the price, not eligibility: the investment thresholds remain US$1,050,000, or US$800,000 in a targeted employment area (TEA) or infrastructure project. Anyone with an I-526/I-526E, an I-829, or a regional-center application (I-956/I-956F) ready, or nearly so, should weigh filing before November 30 to lock in the current fee — remembering that what counts is the postmark, not the date USCIS receives it. After that date, the cost of entering the program — and of maintaining it, for regional centers — becomes the new one.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20016 — EB-5 Fee Rule (final rule, 91 FR 61940, effective 11/30/2026) ↗

Rules & Agencies

Precedent

The Board narrows the appeal again: conjecture is not error, says Matter of K-L-S-

The Board of Immigration Appeals issued a new precedent decision, Matter of K-L-S-, 30 I&N Dec. 241 (BIA 2026), with two weighty rules on what makes an appeal viable. First: presenting hypothetical arguments and suggested alternative views of the evidence is not enough to establish clear error in the Immigration Judge's factual findings — one must point to concrete error in the record, not reopen the evidentiary debate. Second: an appeal based merely on conjecture and speculation lacks an arguable basis in fact or in law, serves no purpose other than to create delay, and is legally inappropriate. The decision reinforces deference to the Judge's fact-finding and arrives in step with the EOIR's new appellate rule: that a thin appeal is, increasingly, dismissed outright. The practical lesson is the same as yesterday's — the evidence and every argument must be built before the Judge, because the appeal is no longer the place to test theories.

Official source: EOIR — Board of Immigration Appeals, Matter of K-L-S-, 30 I&N Dec. 241 (BIA 2026), Interim Decision #4275 ↗
Proposed rule

The 60-day grace period: still in force while DHS decides

The DHS proposal to eliminate the discretionary grace period of up to 60 days (FR 2026-18631) remains under review of the comments, which closed on November 10, with no final rule published. Until the Federal Register carries the final action, the benefit stands: a worker who loses a job in H-1B, L-1, O-1, E-1/E-2/E-3 or TN may remain up to 60 days (or until the end of the authorized stay, if shorter) to change employers or categories. It is the window that allows time to file a new petition — and while there is a risk it may disappear, anyone in those categories should keep a backup plan ready before any layoff.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-18631 — Eliminating the Discretionary 60-Day Grace Period ↗
Proposed rule

Deference to prior approvals: comments closed, decision pending

Also awaiting a DHS decision is the proposal (FR 2026-20941) to rescind 8 CFR 214.1(c)(5) — the rule that today directs USCIS to respect a prior approval when adjudicating extensions of H-1B, L-1, O-1 and other I-129 categories, where the parties and facts are the same; comments closed on November 13. While deference remains in force, each extension still starts from the approval already granted; if the rule becomes final, USCIS may re-examine each request from scratch. The recommended approach does not change: building extension requests with the full evidentiary force of a first petition is the hedge against both scenarios.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20941 — Removing Deference to Prior USCIS Determinations of Eligibility ↗

PERM & Labor Certification

Proposed rule

Comments on the new PERM run through January 19 — and the layoff bar is the point to engage

The PERM modernization proposal (FR 2026-23370, RIN 1205-AC29) is in public comment, and the official deadline to comment runs through January 19, 2027, via regulations.gov, in docket ETA-2026-0133 — which already logs more than five hundred comments. Nothing changes today: 20 CFR Part 656 remains in force. But now is when employers and counsel have a formal voice to show the DOL what works and what makes sponsorship unworkable. The point most deserving of a well-grounded comment is the two-year bar: under the proposal, an employer that has laid off U.S. workers in the same occupation (or a similar occupation) at the worksite in the prior two years is barred from filing, with a duty to consider the laid-off worker through I-140 approval. Anyone planning green cards for 2027 should map the company's layoff history now and use the comment window.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-23370 — Modernizing the Labor Market Test (PERM) ↗ · Comments: regulations.gov, docket ETA-2026-0133 ↗
Final rule

DOL's OALJ: mandatory electronic filing from December 9

While the major PERM overhaul is only beginning to be debated, one certain change is near: on December 9 the DOL's final rule (FR 2026-22761) takes effect, making electronic filing mandatory in proceedings before the Office of Administrative Law Judges (OALJ), with exceptions for good cause and for parties without counsel. The OALJ is where H-1B and H-2A/H-2B enforcement disputes are heard; an employer with a pending case, or at risk of one, needs access to the electronic system configured before that date, or risks missing defense deadlines.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-22761 — OALJ Mandatory Electronic Filing ↗
Watch

Today's clock: PERM still around ten months, and it is what 2027 is planned around

Until the overhaul becomes a final rule, it is the current system that governs the calendar — and the DOL data keep the average around 300 days for PERM cases decided in the latest month, with the prevailing wage (PWD) processing August requests. Adding the wage-determination, recruitment and review stages, an employer should count a little over a year between the PWD request and certification. And there is the deadline that does not forgive: to sustain H-1B extensions beyond the sixth year, the PERM must be filed at least 365 days before the beneficiary exhausts status — a count the proposal's longer recruitment only tightens.

In Perspective

The EB-5 fee seesaw: how a Colorado ruling sent the fees back to 2016

To understand why the November 30 jump looks so steep, look at the back-and-forth of the past two years. In January 2024, in a sweeping fee overhaul, DHS adjusted nearly the entire USCIS schedule at once — EB-5 fees included — using a full cost-recovery model. Investors and regional centers went to court, and on November 12, 2025, in Moody v. Noem, the U.S. District Court for the District of Colorado stayed the EB-5 fees from that overhaul — holding that the EB-5 Reform and Integrity Act of 2022 required the program's fees to come from an EB-5-specific study, not the general schedule. The effect was abrupt: USCIS had to revert, for EB-5, to the amounts in effect before April 2024, which trace back to the 2016 schedule. For about a year, then, the program ran on fees nearly a decade old. The rule taking effect now is the government's answer to that defeat: rebuilt along the path the court pointed to, with the specific fee study Congress had required in 2022. The lesson is the usual one in administrative law — when an agency skips the step the statute commands, the shortcut charges interest: years of litigation, a trip back in time, and, in the end, a larger increase, concentrated on a single day.

Official source: Federal Register 2026-20016 — EB-5 Fee Rule, 91 FR 61940 (recital of Moody v. Noem, 2025 WL 3157554 (D. Colo.) and 89 FR 6194) ↗
